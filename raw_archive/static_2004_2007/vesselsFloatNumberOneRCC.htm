"Float Number 1" is a former Royal Canal horse-drawn barge. Float Number 1 may have had a previous name as a working canal boat on the Royal Canal and only became a "Float" when she was decommissioned from working life and became a support craft to the engineering staff on the Royal Canal. We have been told there were five "Float"s, of which we think she seems to be the only one surviving, indeed maybe the only Royal Canal vessel overall surviving from that era from when she was built in the very lat 1800s.

She came into our hands by we think fateful intervention when we were told about her on a leap day - Sunday 29th February 2004 when we were attending a Kildare IWAI branch gathering at Hazelhatch to launch some Grand Canal 200 celebrations. We were actually on our way from Hazelhatch to look at another barge hull that had been advertised for sale. The fateful comment was something along the lines of "sure why don't you have a look at the hull down the road" - the person who made it thought we could do with seeing what a hull out of the water looked like. So we had a dander and thought mmm, she's lovely.

Having looked at the other hull and with a sinking feeling realising we were falling for the boat on blocks in a yard in Celbridge, Co Kildare (leap day influences, was she calling out to us?) we contacted the owner. Fortune was shining brightly on us and with a helping word or two from a waterways pal or two a few weeks later we were the proud owners/custodians of this fine vessel. With great excitement we showed photos and videos of her to all those interested, and no doubt a few that we bored to tears. The non-boaty people thought we had lost the plot, others who appreciate such things were glad for us. As were we also expecting a new baby a few months later, we caused great confusion when we announced that we had a new arrival to the family.

After the human baby arrived an exciting crane lift and move on the back of a lorry ensued to bring the "Float Number 1" to west Offaly, where she is currently in Shannon Harbour undergoing phase 1 of the refurbishment plan. Those in the know tell us there are many many more phases to go. On the exact moment of the day she was placed back in the canal, there was a very very soft noise like a long 'phew' from the hull. Some have said ah you're romancin', others have said yes that it is the metal stresses resettling as the tensions in the plate shifted when the water moved into the dry dock and lifted her of the plinths. We like to think it was a sign of relief that she was back in her natural habitat. Oh dear - we have the barge bug bad!

We hope to find out more of the history of these type of canal boats as we undertake the project. There is very little paperwork surviving from the era of her construction - we have been told she was built in 1897. There is a slight possibility that she was built in the Ulster Foundry in Portadown, we need to do more research on this. We know her metal is steel and that she is a lovely shape and in pretty good nick for her age and stage. The craft was built of 1/4" plate on 2" x "2 X5 1/6" angle iron at 21" centres, riveted throughout. We were kindly permitted to copy some cine camera footage from the 1960s when she was taken out of the Royal Canal near what was called Liffey Junction in Dublin. Perhaps that is what saved her, she might be long gone except for that. The previous owner's father had purchased her from CIE approx 1964. She was originally 70 foot long but had 10 feet cut out of her so that she could travel through the Grand Canal locks which are shorter than those on the Royal. She is now 60 foot long, 10.5 feet wide and the crane weighed her at 15 tonne on the day of the lift from Celbridge to Shannon Harbour 04 Sept 2004.
