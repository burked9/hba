Aqualegia

Aqualegia is a replica of a Dutch Luxemotor and has been specifically built to travel the inland waterways of Ireland and France. She was designed and built to have all the strengths of the original Luxemotors, with some modern additions and replacements.

Like the originals she has a round stern with pointed bow, a cabin to the rear, engine room with dismountable wheel house on top, a steel bulkhead in front of the engine room and where the deep hold would have been, is further living space. Under the water her hull is a double-ender (pointed at both ends) making it ideal for cruising inland waterways as she handles well in confined waters and creates virtually no wash to disturb the banks and wildlife. The interior was inspired by vessels of the 1920s with painted tongue and groove trimmed with varnished wood.

Since she does not carry freight, there is no need for a mast and boom but ballast is required, which is a combination of brick and water tanks. She has a quiet Beta Marine keel cooled engine which occupies about one fifth of the space needed for the original Brons and Kromhouts of the 1920s.

History of Luxemotors

In 1920 the first Luxemotors were built in the Netherlands and Belgium. The Luxemotor was different from other motorised Dutch barges of that time (like the Beurtschip, Westlandse, Steilsteven and Motorspit) because they were designed specifically to house engines, and not converted to motor power from sail or steam power.

Because of the topography of the Netherlands, the Dutch had extensive experience in building sailing and horse drawn barges for every kind of inland, coastal and offshore waterway. Using this experience and the technology of the 1920s, the Luxemotor hull design was rounded at the stern (kont) and many had the pointed bow (kop) of a river ship with deep cargo holds. They ranged in length from 14 metres with a beam of 2.8 metres and a cargo capacity of 30 tons, to 40 metres by 5 metres with a 250 ton capacity and were designed to travel the canals, rivers, and open waters. The construction was of steel plate and frames which made it easy to extend later if needed. The sea-going versions had plates of 8-10 mm and the inland versions 6-8mm.

They were built with three main sections; Accommodation; Engine Room with Wheelhouse above; and Cargo Hold. On the foredeck was a mast with boom used to load and unload the cargo and in the forepeak there was an extra bunk alcove (vooronder) for the deckhand.

Accommodation: Behind the Wheelhouse was the living cabin (roef). This section had full standing room, with 4-6 windows above deck level, and contained a Toilet and Washroom, a small Galley, a Dining and Sitting area and two Sleeping alcoves under the round stern. When money was available the living space was given a luxurious fit-out using a selection of woods and glass.

Engine Room & Wheelhouse: The lower section was made of steel with the top section built of wood. The top section could be dismantled when passing under low bridges.

The Engine Room was located under the wheelhouse. Engines installed in Dutch barges of this era [for example the Brons and Kromhout] were immense, three metres high with a solid flywheel of 2-3 tons. A large space was needed for the engine, fuel and oil tanks, gearbox and the drive shaft. A metal bulkhead separated the engine room from the cargo space.

To assist in handling, some barges had bow propellers (Kopschroef) and side mounted screw propellers (Zijschroef) powered by Renes engines.

Cargo Hold: With its deep sides, the design of the Luxemotor hold (holte) allowed more freight to be carried than in previous Dutch designs. The hold had to be kept clean and totally watertight. The floor of the hold (buikdenning) was made of boards oiled on the bottom side. Over the hold were numbered hatch boards designed to fit tightly together, with tarred cloths on top lashed down with hasps at the sides to form a weatherproof covering.

Luxemotor Crew

A Luxemotor crew consisted of the Skipper with a deckhand (Knecht) and the Skippers Wife. They earned their living by carrying the manufactured goods of the cities to the people in the countryside and the produce of the countryside to the cities. The barges were usually kept in good condition, being both the family home and a means of livelihood.

Life along the canals of the Netherlands in the late 19th and early 20th century was hard work. Each bridge and lock had a Watcher who was on duty from one hour before sunrise to one hour after sunset. The barge skippers paid at every bridge and lock. This money was used to pay for the Watchers, their houses and to maintain the canals.

Small communities grew up at some of these pay points which made good overnight stops for the skippers and their families after their 12 hour day. This stop was an opportunity to socialise and find out what was going on in their world. Many people did not leave their home towns, so those that did travel like the barge skippers and their families, were respected.

Many barge skippers were the sons and grandsons of skippers and married the daughters of skippers. They needed considerable business acumen to trade and negotiate. As a group they were aware of the advantages of a good education so went to considerable trouble to ensure their children attended school regularly. In an era when schooling was not enforced most 16 year old barge teenagers had a good academic, commercial and practical background in the family enterprise.

When they deemed it appropriate, and with the financial help of their parents, sons of skippers would purchase their own barge, employ a deckhand and start trading.


Luxemotors during WW II

In 1940 the German occupying forces in the Netherlands, Belgium and France impounded all suitable ships and barges for the planned invasion of the British Isles, Operation Sealion. Among those considered suitable for this purpose were the Luxemotors. However, when the orders to seize the relevant barges were issued, many of them were hard to find and if found, were hidden in remote locations. This was not surprising as the skippers were still trying to make some kind of living and giving up their barge also meant the loss of the family home. Because of the shortage of fuel and the commandeering of the motor barges, sailing barges were again used to transport any available goods to and from the countryside.

The motor barges commandeered were modified and strengthened to make them suitable for a sea crossing and carrying a tank and troops, with guns on deck. In the end Operation Sealion did not take place but most of the converted vessels were kept for war work across Western and Eastern Europe and many were destroyed during the war.


Dutch Freight Barges Today

After the war, no more Luxemotors were built. With the continued expansion of the canals and locks on the main waterways of France, Germany, Belgium and the Netherlands, skippers were encouraged to extend their barges or replace them with larger vessels of a more efficient size for freight carrying.

Today the barges used as part of the Netherlands supply chain are huge and compete with other forms of freight transport, travelling the waterways set up to accommodate them. A Eurobarge might load up with 3000 tons of cargo from an ocean going freighter at Rotterdam and travelling throughout the day and night, pass through Germany, Austria, Slovakia, Hungary, Croatia, Bulgaria, Romania, Moldova and on to the Black Sea, distributing their load along the way. On some waterways, you may come across a train of large barges belting along.

However, some of the original 65 to 85 year old Luxemotors still survive carrying their cargos along the smaller waterways of the Netherlands. Many of the smaller ones (25 x 5 metres or less) have been converted for pleasure cruising and living aboard and can be seen on rivers such as the Thames, Shannon, Seine and Rhine and on the canals of Belgium, France, Germany and the Netherlands. Those suitable for the Irish canals (of 4 metres or less abeam), are a rare find.
