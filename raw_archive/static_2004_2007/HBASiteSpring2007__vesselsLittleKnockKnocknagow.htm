Little Knock

Knocknagow: the Little Knock

 



 


 

 

 
 

On board the Knocknagow, but lying loose, is a plate saying that it was built in Portadown in 1930. However, the design of the boat seems to make it much older than that; informed sources suggest 1890s/1900s.
The size of the boat is odd. It was clearly built for inland use, given its straight sides. With its deep stern (currently drawing 4'), it was probably built as a tug-barge. However, its original dimensions seem to have been 67' 6" by 12' 6" (counting a 6" wooden rubbing strake, 3" per side), and they don't match most waterways. If the boat had been built for the Suir, which has no locks, there would be no reason to confine it to 12' 6" beam.
It has been suggested that, with those dimensions, the boat might have been built for the Forth & Clyde Canal, but I have been unable to find information on any craft that used that canal apart from passenger steamers, horse-drawn lighters or puffers. Nonetheless, the word "Scotland" appears in small raised letters on the boat's stern; that might fit with the Forth & Clyde idea.
I have been told that the boat originally had a steam engine, then a Bolinder, finally the present BMC six-cylinder diesel. Could it have gone to Portadown to get its steam engine replaced or to have other work carried out? But why go all that way? It wouldn't have fitted up the Lagan Navigation so would have had to go via the Newry Canal (if it was navigable at the time) or around the north coast to the Lower Bann, then across Lough Neagh.
Knocknagows
Dowleys owned two boats called Knocknagow at the same time, our one (the Little Knock or Little Tug) and another, now sunk up the Nore (the Big Knock or Big Tug). However, Brendan Cleary says that the Big Knock was named Knocknagow I and ours was Knocknagow II. That would suggest that the Big Knock joined the fleet first, but our hull shape seems to be much older than that of the Big Knock. We believe that one Knocknagow joined Dowleys in 1912, but which was it? And if our boat is really from the 1890s or 1900s, where was it before that? The only suggestion I've had came from Jack O'Neill.
Donovans
Jack O'Neill, the Waterford railway historian, says that the Donovan family operated lighters in Waterford from the early 1900s. They carried goods between the railway goods yard and the ships along the quays. They also carried limestone. It seems that Co Wexford has little limestone, so lighters brought it from quarries at Kilmacow up the Barrow to New Ross and other quays, from which carts brought it further. He said that the Knocknagow was used in that business, although it probably had a different name at that time.
The opening of the Waterford to Rosslare Railway in 1906 provided competition for the delivery of limestone, and Donovans eventually sold out of that business and bought a pub on the quays. They also sold the Knocknagow. After the Civil War, they also left the other lighterage business; later generations were in the motor trade and one family member was a solicitor; one descendant may still be alive.
John Sexton said
I remember Donovans; until about ten years ago [1996] Joe Donovan had a truck repair business just beside the Mansion House pub, beside the Infirmary (now apartments). Joe also ran a sand & gravel business formerly from the same premises that backs onto Johns Pill, the little river that runs up past where the gas works used to be. I know that coal was brought to the gas works by lighter & it's only another 300m to where the sand was sold, so undoubtedly it was brought in by barge at an earlier time. Joe & the brother Kevin were characters: Joe always ran a Mercedes car, even 30 years ago! Another guy in the trade described Joe as having "Merc Fever", which condition required that the sufferer possess a "Merc Cheque Book". Donovan's yard still exists as a car sales patch. JS.
The Suir navigation
In 1877 Messrs John Grubb & Son (MD J Ernest Grubb) put the tug Father Mathew on the Waterford to Carrick-on-Suir run. It could tow four lighters to a maximum of 160 tons and could carry passengers to fairs in Waterford. Grubb was a Quaker; he named his tug after the temperance crusader as he too opposed misuse of alcohol.
River transport between Waterford and Carrick (sand, coal, corn, timber) was based on unpowered lighters that used the tide to ascend and descend the Suir. At one stage there were hundreds of men in the trade; according to William O'Callaghan, his family's lighters were pitch pine, 71' X 16'. The crew of two used 30' steel-shod poles for manoeuvring at close quarters; for steerage, to keep in the tidal stream, they each had a 35' or 36' sweep, each stroke of which meant walking six steps forward and six back. The oars swivelled on 2" oak dowels.
There was a separate horse-drawn trade from Carrick to Clonmel, with up to thirteen horses used at times by a crew of two; lighters and smaller 16-ton yawls were used, with the yawl tied aft on the Waterford side to do the steering.

Tarpeys and Dowleys
Grubb's tug threatened the lighter trade and his business eventually controlled much of the river trade. He retired in 1912 and his Suir Steam Navigation Company was bought by Thomas Walsh & Son in June of that year. They continued the trade to Clonmel until 1920 or 1921; in 1927 they sold the Father Mathew to the Tarpey family of Carrick Beg (the town on the Waterford, south side of the river) but sold the Waterford-Carrick business to Edward J Dowley & Sons (who had bought the Grubb stores at New Street in Carrick).
The Dowleys fleet
It is possible that Dowleys did not take the opportunity to buy the Father Mathew in 1927 because, in 1912, they had bought a tug of their own, the Knocknagow, for £1,600. Dr Patrick C Power says that its first skipper was J Healy and that the cost "was recouped quickly by towing sand-boats that collected river-sand for Waterford builders". It could carry about 40 tons, which matches the capacity of our boat and is too small for the Big Knock.
Dowleys eventually owned four boats altogether. Apart from the 1912 Knocknagow, they were:
· the New Forge, which carried a little over 60 tonnes and was described as more like a canal boat, being blunt in front. It had a two-man crew and a Gardner engine that was notoriously hard to start: handles at the two ends of the engine had to be swung in opposite ways. The New Forge is currently based in Shannon Harbour
· the Rocksand, which may have been called Corsand at some stage, and which had a single-cylinder Plenty-Still engine and a three-man crew. It carried 200 tonnes (usually grain downriver, fertiliser up) and was the least-used of the Dowleys fleet and the first to be sold off. It berthed at their downstream yard, along with the lighters, and was used for storage when not required for transport. It was used in the dismantling of the old New Ross bridge in 1968 and was bought by Dickie Fletcher for his New Ross Galley cruising restaurants: now without its engine, it is moored above the bridge in New Ross as the floating pontoon base, with kitchens and offices
· the Knocknagow I, also known as the Big Knock and the Big Tug. Dr Power believes that this boat (which he described as diesel driven, 89.6 tonnes) was bought in the 1930s to replace the 1912 Knocknagow, but other evidence shows that both boats were in service with Dowleys at the same time, with the newer vessel given the lower number. Brendan Cleary, who worked for Dowleys, said that the Big Knock carried a 60-tonne cargo, had a three-man crew and was powered by a two-cylinder Bolinder diesel engine, using compressed air to start the pre-heated engine; the engine had to be stopped to change from forward to reverse and vice versa. Dr Power says the boat was sold in 1973; William O'Callaghan says it carried 80-85 tonnes, had very thin plates and was sold to one Willie Hanlon, who sold it to Neddie Suffin for half nothing. Suffin, a haulier, used it to draw sand from the river and then sold it to someone on the Nore, where it lies sunk. However, the Munster Express in April 2002 said that Walter O'Keeffe of Polerone, Mooncoin, and his brother, had used the Knocknagow, with a crane on board, to draw sand from the river and bring it to Polerone Quay, whence it was sold to builders.
Dowleys sold out to Suttons in 1982; Suttons was subsequently acquired by IAWS, although it sold the Carrick operation (and two others) to its Cork Managing Director, who set up Goldcrop. The Knocknagow I seems to have been the last boat to carry goods on the Suir, in 1973, but sand-dredging continued until 1983, when William O'Callaghan's sand barge ceased operations.
The Little Knocknagow
It seems likely, but is not proven, that ours is the boat that Dowleys bought in 1912; it matches the specification cited by Dr Power. When employed by Dowleys it was known as the Knocknagow II, the Little Tug or the Little Knock.
The steam-engine was replaced at some stage - perhaps in 1930 at Portadown - by a 15hp Bolinder, with compressed-air starting of the pre-heated engine (glow plugs?). [William O'Callaghan said it was 60hp.] The throttle could be controlled from the helm but an engineer was still required in the engine-room.
Crew
The boat had a three-man crew: the skipper/helmsman, the engineer (who had to stay in the engine-room) and the deck hand. According to Dr Power, the first skipper was one J Healy. Apart from J Healy, there were at least five other skippers:
· Mikey Dowley (pronounced Dooley)
· Johnny Norris, who skippered both Knocknagows. He was Chairman of the Carrick Urban District Council in 1947; he is said to have grounded on a shoal once in order to reach a Council meeting on time. According to Dr Power, "he championed the rights of the workers of the town and as a skilful boatman, [...] distinguished himself in the annual regattas." His son Tom says that he was skipper for about twenty years. He was let go from Dowleys and he drowned on 27 December 1949
· Tim Sweeney from Carrick Beg, who later worked the gantry hoisting the cargo to and from Dowley's store
· Tom Sweeney, brother of Tim
· Paddy Sweeney, son of Tim, of 40 St Mollerans, Carrick Beg. He had been a deck hand.
William O'Callaghan said that Pat Reilly was originally in charge of the steam engine but was sacked by Joe Dowley, who wanted a younger man. He said that James Jacques, of Friary Height, was engineer for over forty years on both Knocknagows, covering both the steam and the diesel era. James Jacques died on 22 January 1958 when, in winter floods, the boat got stuck in the wooden supports of the bridge at Fiddown. While trying to pry it free with a bar, he slipped and fell in to the river. Although he managed to swim to the shore, the cold caused his heart to give way. If he was engineer for over forty years, he must have started in about 1917.
Sean Barrett, a tall (6' 3"), thin man, replaced James Jacques as engineer.
Brendan Cleary, who retired in July 2002 after forty-six years service with Dowleys, Suttons and Goldcrop, worked at the stores in Carrick. Tony Mullins worked for Dowleys and became manager under Suttons; after Goldcrop was set up, he started a garden centre. John Keating was another Dowleys employee.
Forty to fifty people worked in Dowleys altogether; seven trucks made deliveries as far afield as North Tipperary, Wexford and North Kilkenny and there were depots at Cashel, Clonmel, Callan and Waterford, as well as a Fiat agency in Thurles.
Dowleys in Carrick
The Carrick poet Michael Coady refers to the area between the two bridges as the Town Pond. The Dowleys stores were on the North Quay, where two loading gantries overhung the river, and Knocknagow's berth was at the foot of Oven Lane. The quays were concreted during World War II.
High water-levels often flooded the premises, and the office (as well as the boats) had to get copies of the Waterford tide tables each January. Every night barrow-loads of yellow clay would be used to block the doors and there was an automatic underground pump to drain from the yard to the river.
A child playing on the moored Knocknagow was said to have fallen in to the river and drowned. Mary McGrath of UCD said that his mother was from Czechoslovakia and that he was a golden-haired boy. The family name may have been Mullins.
Dowleys had a second store downriver (Ballylynch?) in a backwater; Rocksand, the lighters and the concrete barges were based here. A crane with a bucket, sometimes using slings, emptied phosphates and potash from the boats and conveyed them to the store where Dowleys blended their fertilisers (4.4.8 and 0.5.10), for sale in 2cwt jute bags, well into the 1960s. Although it was a small plant, it despatched 50 to 60 tons per day, selling both wholesale and retail.
An outflow pipe here has short arms (skips?) at right angles to it on which a vessel could take the ground at high tide; the bottom could then be inspected when the tide went out, and Petey Walsh of Precision Engineering in Carrick could weld on plates when necessary. The facility was used by Dickie Fletcher of the New Ross Galley - after he had had a dragline clear it out.
Daily operations
The two Knocknagows (and the New Forge?) travelled the Suir almost every day, missing out only when held up, eg if shipping had not come in. Leaving Carrick at 07.00, if the tide was right they would be moored in Waterford by 09.30 or 10.00. That meant the crews started work at 06.00; if they were back in Carrick before 19.00, they got no overtime.
The boats had dark red hulls, with their names in white, green cabins and black decks. The gantry crane would lift forty gallons of diesel to fill each of them, which would get them to Waterford and back (***seems a lot of diesel***), as well as a bag of coal for the stove in the bows in case they had to sleep overnight. The New Forge was said to be particularly comfortable.
William O'Callaghan said that the Little Knocknagow was regarded more as a tug than as a cargo-carrier: being very deep aft, the propellor was never out of the water, even when empty, so the boat was good at towing, and would often give a tow to unpowered sand and gravel boats.
Jack O'Neill said that Knocknagow towed both non-engined barges and sand barges. It would discharge sand on the Waterford side of the river and return to Carrick next day towing a barge laden with general cargo, at two miles an hour.
Wheat was one of the major cargoes carried from Carrick to Waterford: two hundred tons might be loaded in a single day. It would have been kept in sacks in Dowley's store (120' X 60'), with each farmer's lot marked and then accounted for when it was put into the boats. Flour was carried loose in the hold and sucked out at the mills in Waterford. When loading, the crane on the gantry would lift four or six sacks out over the boat and lower them. Two men would stand on planks arranged over the hold, lift the sacks off the slings (???), empty them into the hold and send the sacks back. Brendan Cleary had to report to Mr Willie Dowley immediately the boat was loaded, listing the number of sacks and the gross weight from each farmer. Those reports were later checked against the returns from the Waterford mills. (Waterways World had an article illustrating the use of snotters on the sacks, as described by Brendan Cleary.)
The crew were paid 1.5d tonnage money for bulk wheat to Waterford and 2.5d for bagged wheat. Some would ask Kitty in the office to give them two envelopes, one with the ordinary pay and overtime for their wives and one with the extras for themselves. Brendan Cleary was responsible for the log sheets, time sheets and stock sheets.
Concrete barges
Dowleys acquired one or two concrete barges after World War II (or World War I: see below): one was said to be "a large and cumbersome craft, 100 ft long, 24 ft in the beam and 13 ft high". It was later used to shore up a breached embankment. Mick Byrne believes that the other barge was buried at Pollrone Quay near Mooncoin to shore up a breached embankment. He photographed the second, referred to as Cretefield, in 1988 at the CAP factory, which was later developed into Bellview port.

Knocknagow on the Slaney
Roadstone
Roadstone Wexford took over a quarry at Brownswood on the River Slaney, two miles below Enniscorthy, from the County Council. Washed aggregates (gravel) were being bought in from a local supplier, but Roadstone felt it could get them from the bed of the river, already washed. Accordingly, in the mid-1960s it bought the Knocknagow and sailed it around the south-east coast to Brownswood Jetty.
Nick O'Donnell, an inter-county hurler, managed the conversion from barge to dredger, placing a mechanical shovel on the bow; up to 100 tons were extracted daily. The crew was John Myles and Willie Kavanagh.
Richard Miller
In 1975 Roadstone decided to scrap the Knocknagow and sold it to O'Rourke Scrap Merchants. However, Richard Miller, a Wexford farmer, saved it from the scrap heap, but had to promise that the boat would not be used in the sand and gravel trade but would be converted for pleasure purposes.
He actually converted it twice and, in the process, shortened it by 6' 6" so that it would fit in the locks of the Grand Canal. He beached the boat on the slipway of the former US flying-boat station in Wexford, cut out a section from the middle and then joined it up again. He also added a wheelhouse. That gave a length of 61', beam 12' 6", draught 4', air draught 9'. He did bring the boat to the Shannon once, but getting back was a slow process.
He reconditioned the engine in 1976 but also decided to enable it to sail, so he fitted it with two masts, with a specially-made 24' wide X 13' deep square sail on the foremast, with a 6' bonnet, and a gaff and a topsail on the main, with leeboards. In 1986/87 he overplated the bottom with 5mm plate. This version was advertised for sale in 1988, for £32,000, but was not sold. The specification included
· BMC 5.1 litre 6 cylinder diesel. Borg Warner 71C gear box 2:1 reduction. Gear box cooler, engine oil cooler, heat exchanger. 2 Jabsco pumps. 24V Dynamo, 1000W. Lister 1.9hp diesel driving capstan and winch
· forecastle: store with capstan engine etc, large vice
· saloon: open plan, 8 berths, 2 large lockers. Solid fuel heater
· galley: lockers, sink, worktops, 2-burner gas cooker, grill, oven
· marine toilet, shower well
· central heating and hot water from engine
· road trailer available; delivery negotiable.
The Knocknagow made some coastal trips (one to West Cork and one to Scotsman's Bay near Dun Laoghaire), went upriver on Slaney rallies and was used as a mother-ship for Wexford Harbour Boat and Tennis Club regattas. But the first conversion went on fire and Richard rebuilt the boat, this time with a low cabin and no wheelhouse.
He also brought the boat home - three tractors to haul it up the slipway in Wexford on the trailer he had built for it; two to carry it through the streets and down the winding roads to his farm - and replated it in 6mm steel in 1994, removing the old plating from the inside and adding new frames. A hydraulic centreboard was installed and the inside of the hull was sprayed with insulating polyurethane foam down to the water line.
The boat was surveyed on 19 May 1998 and 15 May 2001 by Carrolls Boatyard, Ballyhack. When offered for sale in 2004 at 35,750 the specification included
· 61' X 12' X 4' 6" X 8'
· 90hp BMC; Borg Warner 71C 2:1 reduction gearbox
· manual chain-link steering
· 2 X 200-gallon diesel tanks
· 24v. Wired for shore power
· davits on stern.
The sails were included but not the masts. John Lefroy's survey report of 10 March 2004 said inter alia
The vessel has flat fore and aft decks, a side-deck with a step two-thirds of hull-length aft, and a steel coachroof amidships over the old hold. Hull-form is full-displacement, with a flat bottom, a round turn-of-bilge, a straight stem and a counter-stern. ... Integral water-tanks were fitted from the for'd bulkhead aft through the galley, etc in two sections, effectively providing a double bottom.
A 2" S/S propeller-shaft emerges through a cutlass-bearing held in a steel tube securely welded into the aft end of the hull: in order. A 21" x 19" bronze three-bladed turbine propeller is swung: in good condition and well-fixed by nut and safety-pin. The stern-post carries a large steel rudder on a steel stock; this is well-supported by upper and lower strap-bearings and all in good condition.
Knocknagow on the Shannon
Knocknagow was sold by Richard Miller in 2004. The handover was at Vicarstown on the Barrow Line of the Grand Canal.
Richard skippered the boat, with crew from both old and new owners, from Wexford to St Mullins on Good Friday, 9 April 2004, leaving at 10.30 and reaching St Mullins at 20.45. It maintained 1900 RPM all the way, except while picking up passengers at Ballyhack, and reached 8.5 knots coming up the estuary. Carnsore was abeam at 12.00, Hook Head at 16.25 and New Ross at 19.07.
Because Knocknagow draws more water than the Barrow provides, Richard devised a method of reducing the draft by securing three air-filled forty-gallon barrels under each side of the counter stern. That lifted the boat by nine inches and, while it scraped a few rocks on the way upriver, it suffered no damage. The barrels were secured by 11 April and the boat reached Vicarstown on 16 April for the handover.
After a period of commissioning at Vicarstown, the boat reached the Main Line of the Grand Canal in time to join the Heritage Boat Association fleet heading westward to Shannon Harbour, and reached the Shannon at the end of June 2004. It is now based at Dromineer on Lough Derg.
Much work has been done on the engine and there is some new equipment, but the basic design is still as Richard Miller made it. Accommodation below decks is less than might be expected, because of the forecastle and the large engine room, but the boat has a lot of deck space.
Michael Coady wrote in The Nationalist of 22 August 1998: "I recall still summer evenings when you'd hear the distinctive knock-knock-knock of the Knocknagow's engine before she'd appear through the Navigation Cut down river and sail up under the New Bridge, into the Town Pond to her berth beside the quay at the foot of Oven Lane." The boat is still fondly remembered by many Carrick people, some of whom have been surprised to meet it on the canal or the Shannon.
More sources
Bill Irish says the Waterford Custom House register from 1877 to 1977 is on microfilm, but you'd have to know the name of a boat to trace it. The harbour board's records are with the National Archives. Mick Byrne looked at the register but found no trace. Terry Ó Conláin says that Irish vessels had to be on the British register up to (and perhaps after) WW2. He says that the UK authorities culled their registers, keeping only every tenth year, and that the other years are now in St John's Newfoundland. He looked at his records but could find no mention of Knocknagow in the registers including those of 1892 and 1930. He says some Grand Canal hack boats, sold to Carrick-on-Suir, had been built in Inverkeating in Scotland, but that doesn't seem to connect with anything.
