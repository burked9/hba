Dabu**            Back to [Vessels](vesselb.htm) | | |  |  | | *Dabu south of Carrick* |  | | No.2 DABU Built in 1878, Canal Boat No.2 is the second iron boat ever built for the Grand Canal Company. Probably built by Bewley & Webb, Dublin who built most of the early iron trade boats, she would have cost in the region of £300. 613" in length and 133" wide, she is built of 3/8inch Swedish iron, and her ribs, which are 21/2" x 21/2" angle iron are at 2 centres. Everything is held together with "hot rivets" which are 2" apart. She was built in the traditional style of all ships, laid from her keel upwards.  Her pointed nose, rounded stern and her keel allow her to be moved easily through the water, as No.2 was designed as a horse boat and never had an engine in the trading days.  Registered in 1907 as No.2 in Killaloe, she would have travelled the canal system extensively in her early years. Including the Ballinasloe Canal and the River Barrow which were the motorways of Ireland at that time. As with todays lorries she would have carried all types of cargo, from manure, turf, coal and gravel to flour, wheat, Guinness and other general groceries.  No.2, as with all the other horse boats of the time, had a crew of 3, consisting of a captain, a deckhand and a horseman*.* The Captains quarters were at the stern of the boat and contained one bed and a closed fire. The crews quarters were at the bow and this room had 2 beds and a stove, which was more than likely a pot-bellied stove.  She ended her working days as a dumb barge carrying muck and dirt etc. on the building of Ardnacrusha power station in the 1940s and early 50s. Construction completed, she lay abandoned to a watery grave opposite the hotel in Killaloe after approximately 75 years of service.  *P**ic 1*  ***THE REVIVAL***  My late father, Sean Bayly & my mother Brigid Bayly, in the early 60s, used to sail from Port Oriel, Clogher Head to the Ringsend Docks. From there they would travel the length of the Grand Canal to the Shannon for the annual summer holidays.  (Remember, there were still trading boats on the canal at this time)  It was on one of these early trips on the Shannon that they noticed No.2 sunk in the reed beds in Killaloe (see pic 1). They fell in love with her elegant design, she was unlike the other trade boats on the canal, which were flat nosed and flat bottomed, and dreamt about owning her some day.  On a later cruise to Killaloe, they noticed her resurrected and afloat. Immediately they inquired of the owner.  The owner was the late John Weaving, who at that time worked for Emerald Star in Carrick-on-Shannon at the weekends. With haste they turned their twin masted, gaff rigged sailing yacht, Barracuda for Carrick-on-Shannon to see John Weaving.  A price of £80 for No.2 with the condition that she was delivered to Shannon Harbour (see picture below), and No.2 became a trusted member of the Bayly family in August of 1966. You now had one very proud and happy couple, and some might say stupid as their pride and joy Barracuda and a holiday home in Clogher Head had now to be sold to finance a very large pile of iron!      ***SHANNON HARBOUR TO 9TH LOCK CLONDALKIN***  Since No.2 never had an engine, and all her horses had long since retired! A 5hp long shaft Seagull outboard was fixed to her rudder, which my father nicknamed "a barn door" (as it was made of 9" oak and approx 4 wide by 5 deep) and he and his young family headed for Belmont in the winter of 1966.  Weeds, weeds and more weeds! Progress was, however, pathetically slow as the propeller needed cleaning every 5 minutes or less. At this rate it took 2 long days to reach Belmont, 33rd lock, and a change of plan was called for.  What we needed was the Heavy Metal Gang!  Paddy Wilkinson was called for. At that time he owned the Snark and ran her as a restaurant, with Anna Fitzsimmons as cook, at the 9th lock. He was also the owner of 76M with her 15hp Bolinder engine. Paddy and 76M finally arrived at the turning-circle in Belmont in late October to tow No.2 to Clondalkin.  Again progress was very slow due to canal condition, weeds and double locking.  In particular the Robertstown level caused great hardship with high crosswinds and an ice covered canal. This is where I learned my apprenticeship as a greaser on the old bolinder engine. It was extremely hard work for a young lad of 10 years old. 4 weeks later No.2 reached Clondalkin where she was to be transformed.      ***CONVERSION***  All hands on deck! Every person we knew was conscripted to help out with the conversion from open iron hull to 12 berth cruiser and an engine was sought.  A Perkins S6M, 120hp, 6 cylinder diesel was bought from an ex Lord Mayor of Dublin and astern tube had to be installed, as this was No.2s first ever engine, and this was done between the 10th and 11th locks. I, myself got the job of drilling 1000s of 3/16th inch holes in all the ribs to take battens.  The new look No.2, with superstructure and engine was heading west for the Shannon, on her maiden voyage within 8 months.    ***THE NAME***  No.2 needed a name, "a short name" my father stated. Owing to the yearly laborious task of sign writing *Marline* and *Barracuda* on his previous vessels, a short name would minimise the problems.  This was a few weeks before his birthday and in writing him a birthday card, my youngest sister spelt daddy as DABU, and so the name was found!  Interestingly, the word Dabu was found, in later years, to be a dutch word with the translation meaning standing upright Very appropriate!  ***THE 70s***  Dabu became a dual purpose vessel in the 1970s, both as family cruiser and as the latest passenger cruise vessel on the Shannon under B-Line Luxury Cruises  Sleeping up to 10 guests, Dabu with her crew of 3 offered passengers a full Irish breakfast, morning coffee, 4 course lunch, afternoon tea, 4 course dinner & supper while she cruised the waterways, and all this for the paltry sum of £12.50 for a weekend. (see pic 3)      *Pic 3*    ***THE 80s***  Dabu retired from working life (again) and returned to the easy living of a family cruiser. This did not however mean that she ceased travelling the canals and rivers of Ireland, and she could still be seen visiting the many lovely harbours on the waterways system with her young crew. So much so that one lock-keeper (shall remain nameless) has nicknamed her the "mobile crèche."  My father, Sean Bayly died in 1984 at the dry-dock in Shannon Harbour as Dabu was in for her yearly maintenance work. As a result Dabu sat lonely in Shannon Harbour for the next few years, falling into a state of disrepair.  *Dabus visit to IWAI harbour Carrick on Shannon*  **1988  A NEW ERA**  I purchased Dabu from my mother, Bridie Bayly, and a major overhaul ensued. Friends and relatives were again conscripted to help and the old timber superstructure was totally replaced with steel over the winter months.  A second generation of boating aboard Dabu was thus ignited and she has travelled the length and breath of our waterways ever since.  No.2 has spanned three centuries, and seen in two millenniums in her lifetime. She is probably the longest family owned Canal Boat on the waterways, and has inspired a love of the waterways and boating in many of her guests over the years. She is a symbol of hope for the future, that in the next century our waterway system will still remain open for her and her crew to enjoy as it has in the past 100 years and that it will continue to be freely accessible to all our future generations  On this quest, she has struggled as far east as Dublin, west as Limerick and shall accompany a fleet of heritage craft as far north as Enniskillen last year (2004). May she and all who board her travel safely.  © Robert Bayly | | |
